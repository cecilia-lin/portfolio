The question

Are recipes with a higher proportion of calories from sugar rated differently? Can recipe attributes help predict a recipe’s rounded average rating?

Context & data

The published analysis describes 83,782 recipes and 731,927 user interactions from Food.com. Joining and cleaning the data produced 234,429 analysis rows; those rows are not all unique recipes.

The investigation distinguishes individual user ratings from recipe-level average ratings, and treats zero ratings as missing rather than a genuine score.

My contribution

I co-authored the analysis with Angela Hu, working across cleaning, exploratory analysis, missingness assessment, permutation testing, feature engineering, and Random Forest modeling. We translated the investigation into a public, step-by-step write-up.

Approach

  1. Make the data usable. Join recipes to interactions, parse nutrition values, standardize dates, and derive sugar proportion and dessert indicators.
  2. Question missingness. Test whether missing ratings vary with observed recipe characteristics.
  3. Test an association. Use a directional permutation test comparing mean ratings for higher-sugar recipes and the remaining recipes.
  4. Build and improve a model. Start with sugar proportion and dessert status, then add preparation time, calories, and submission year. Tune Random Forest depth and tree count with GridSearchCV.

Evaluation & outcome

0.87Reported baseline F1
0.92Reported tuned-model F1

The permutation analysis found a small difference in ratings: higher-sugar recipes were rated lower in this sample. The write-up reports a p-value below 0.01.

Model evaluation also looked beyond the aggregate score. Lower rating classes remained harder to predict, and precision differed between lower- and higher-calorie groups.

Limitations

This observational analysis shows an association; it does not establish that sugar causes a lower rating or reveal reviewers’ motivations. Missingness and the skew toward high ratings affect interpretation.

The original write-up gives slightly different permutation p-values in its test and conclusion, so this summary reports the common threshold rather than implying additional precision. Its aggregate F1 averaging method is not specified in the narrative; class-level results provide important context.
